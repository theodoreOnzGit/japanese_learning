# People and Politeness

Referring to yourself and others, family terms, handling compliments.
The keigo *verb* ladder lives in `gifts_and_giving.md`; this file covers how you
position **people** rather than actions.

Status labels: `new` · `shaky` · `solid` · `mastered` — see `MAP.md`.

## Language

### Core (N5)

#### Family terms: yours vs theirs — `solid`

Got right unprompted 2026-08-01 (父にもらいました, not お父さん) — the first
clean spontaneous application of the 内/外 rule. Your own family is 内 (uchi), so
you use the humble term when speaking to an outsider.

| Own (humble, to outsiders) | Someone else's (honorific) |
|---|---|
| 父 ちち | お父さん おとうさん |
| 母 はは | お母さん おかあさん |
| 兄 あに / 姉 あね | お兄さん / お姉さん |
| 妻 つま / 夫 おっと | 奥さん / ご主人 |

**Catch:** you *address* your own father as お父さん to his face. The humble form
is only for referring to him when talking to someone outside the family.

Needs one more spaced, spontaneous use to reach `mastered`.

#### Responding to a compliment — `shaky`

Flagged 2026-08-01 — for "your wallet is nice," the user proposed
「ありがとうございました。父にもらいました」. The 父 half was right; the tense
was not.

**ありがとうございます** — a compliment is happening *right now*, so it's
non-past. See `presenting_and_formal.md` for the full ございます/ございました
split (this counts as one of its two misses).

Upgrades offered:
- **父から**もらいました — から is a clearer "from" than に.
- **父にもらったんです** — explanatory んです ("the reason it's nice is that my
  dad gave it to me"). What a native speaker would most likely say. N4.

Full form: ありがとうございます。父にもらったんです。

### Stretch (N4)

#### First-person pronouns: 僕 / 私 / 俺 / わたくし — `new`

From class chat 2026-07-18, via the insight that 僕 is a self-lowering choice
like the humble giving/receiving verbs. Unlike English's single "I," Japanese
makes you pick, and the choice encodes register plus self-positioning.

| Pronoun | Who / register | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| わたくし | most formal (any gender) | very humble/polite — business, ceremony |
| わたし (私) | neutral polite (any gender) | safe default; the formal choice for men too |
| ぼく (僕) | male, softer casual-polite | mild/humble — kanji 僕 = "servant" (しもべ), so it literally lowers the self |
| おれ (俺) | male, rough/casual | assertive, masculine; in-group only, rude to a superior |

- **僕 = "servant" etymology** — choosing it is a self-lowering act, the same
  謙譲 instinct behind いただく / さしあげる.
- **僕 vs 俺** — same meaning, opposite positioning: 僕 softens, 俺 asserts. 俺 to
  a boss is a misstep, same category as あげる to a senior.
- **僕 is male-coded** — a female study partner would use わたし, not 僕.
- **あたし — sensei's warning (2026-07-18):** あたし = わたし with the わ softened;
  very casual and feminine. Sensei said it sounds **"act cute" (ぶりっこ)** and
  advised against it. Fuller picture: not rude, and many women use it naturally,
  but it's **casual-only + carries a girlish connotation**, and is wrong in formal
  settings. Safe rule: default to **わたし** (genderless, never wrong); treat
  あたし as a marked, casual-cutesy choice — same logic as 俺 for men.
  わたし and 僕 are the "safe, unmarked" pronouns.

## Cultural

### Accepting vs deflecting compliments — `new`

From the wallet exchange 2026-08-01. Japanese has a strong norm of **modest
deflection (謙遜, けんそん)** rather than straight acceptance. A flat "thank you"
isn't rude, but it is noticeably more Western/direct.

- **Deflect (traditional, always safe):** いえいえ、そんなことないです。
- **Accept + deflect (very common now):** ありがとうございます。でも、…
- **Redirect the credit (what the user did instinctively):**
  ありがとうございます。父にもらったんです。 — accepts the thanks but assigns the
  merit elsewhere (the wallet is nice because *dad* chose it, not because I have
  great taste). Threads the needle, and natural among friends and younger speakers.

Why it matters: the modesty norm is the same 謙譲 instinct behind humble verbs
and humble family terms. Praising your own possessions or taste too readily reads
as immodest. Deflecting a compliment on *your family member* follows the same
logic (息子は全然だめで… even when proud).

Register note: with close friends a plain ありがとう, or a happy
そう？ありがとう！, is fine — the deflection norm tightens as formality rises.
