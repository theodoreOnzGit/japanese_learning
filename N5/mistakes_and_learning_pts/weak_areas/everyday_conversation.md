## Language

### Core (N5)

#### いいです is ambiguous — accept or refuse — `new`

Flagged 2026-08-01 from the user's own はい、いいですよ (which is correct — see
below). **いいです alone can mean both "yes, that's fine" and "no thank you."**
Responding to an *offer*, the refusal reading is the more common one.

> 「コーヒー、いかがですか。」 → 「いいです。」 = **"No thanks."** — not "yes please"

Same with けっこうです. Convenience-store staff hear いいです as a refusal all day.

What disambiguates: **はい** in front commits it to affirmative; **よ** points
toward granting rather than refusing; and context (answering a *request* vs an
*offer*).

| Intent | Unmistakable version |
|---|---|
| Yes, go ahead | はい、どうぞ / はい、いいですよ |
| No thanks | いいえ、けっこうです / だいじょうぶです |

The user's はい、いいですよ is natural and friendly — granting permission with よ
adding a reassuring "it's fine, really." Fine for a peer or classmate.

#### ほんとに / ほんとうに (本当に) — `new`

Used correctly by the user 2026-08-01 (はい、ほんとにいいですよ — "yes, it's
really fine"), reassuring someone who is checking.

**ほんと**に is the clipped, colloquial form of **ほんとう**に (本当に, "really /
truly"). Both are correct; the long form is what you write, the short form is
what you say. Mixing casual ほんとに with polite ですよ is completely normal in
speech — the politeness lives in the ending, not the adverb.

Related: **本当ですか？**（ほんとうですか）= "Really?" — the standard surprised
response, and a natural partner to へえ (see interjections below).

The ほんとう→ほんと clipping is a general colloquial pattern, same as
すみません→すいません and やはり→やっぱり.

#### と vs や — "and" for nouns — `new`

From class 2026-08-01. Both join **nouns only** — never verbs or adjectives.

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **と** | exhaustive — "A and B", that's the complete list |
| **や** | representative — "A and B, among others" |

> 机の上に 本**と** ペンが あります。→ a book and a pen. Nothing else.
> 机の上に 本**や** ペンが あります。→ a book, a pen, and other things too.

- や often closes with **など** ("etc."): 本**や** ペン**など**が あります。
- Casual spoken equivalent: **とか** — 本**とか** ペン**とか**。
- Verbs chain with て-form; い-adjectives with 〜くて (see
  `describing_and_sentence_building.md`).

#### こちら / そちら / あちら / どちら — the polite こそあど — `new`

From class 2026-07-25, listed among "vocab that eluded me." These are the
**polite/formal** row of the こそあど grid — used for directions, places, and
(politely) for people.

| Casual | Polite | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ここ | **こちら** | here / this way |
| そこ | **そちら** | there / that way |
| あそこ | **あちら** | over there |
| どこ | **どちら** | where / which way |

Two extra jobs beyond "direction":

- **Politely referring to a person:** こちらは田中さんです ("This is Tanaka-san")
  — introducing someone. Never ここ for a person.
- **Polite "which" between two options:** AとBとどちらが〜ですか — the question
  form of より comparison (`shopping.md`).

Shop and service staff default to these forms: こちらへどうぞ ("this way,
please"). The こ/そ/あ/ど logic is the same as これ/それ/あれ/どれ — see `N5.tex`
→ Grammar → Demonstratives for that.

#### わかりました (past) vs わかりません (present) — `new`

Flagged 2026-07-18 — confusing that "I understand" uses PAST わかりました. Key:
**わかる is a change-of-state verb** ("to become clear"), so it marks the *moment
the lightbulb turns on*.

| Meaning | Japanese | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| "I get it / understand now" | わかりました | past ✅ |
| "I don't understand" | わかりません | present ✅ |

*Not*-understanding is an **ongoing state** (lightbulb still off), not a completed
event — hence present, not ~~わかりませんでした~~. The past-for-yes /
present-for-no asymmetry is the whole puzzle.

**Why English feels flexible but Japanese doesn't:** English "understand" is
**stative**, so present tense naturally means an ongoing state. Japanese わかる is
**change-of-state**, so its plain non-past does NOT mean "I understand now":

| English | Japanese | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| "Got it" (just clicked) | わかりました (past) | the realisation moment ✅ |
| "I understand it" (ongoing) | わかっています (te-iru) | lasting state of knowing ✅ |
| — (avoid for "I get it now") | わかります | "will/can understand," or "it's understandable" ❌ |

**Generalises to a whole verb family** — many change-of-state verbs take PAST
form for a PRESENT feeling:

| Feeling (now) | Japanese (past!) | Literally |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm tired" | 疲れました (つかれました) | "became tired" |
| "I'm hungry" | おなかが すきました | "stomach became empty" |
| "I get it" | わかりました | "became clear" |
| "I'm lost" | 道に まよいました | "became lost" |

Ongoing states use ている instead: 知っています, 結婚しています, 座っています.
Two buckets: **just-happened change → past**; **ongoing state → ています**.

Same 完了 logic as ありがとうございました (`presenting_and_formal.md`).

#### だめ (駄目) — `new`

From class 2026-07-18. "No good, not allowed, must not, hopeless."

- **"No / not allowed":** だめです — refusing or forbidding, blunter than いいえ.
- **"No good / broken":** このペンはだめです.
- Pairs with 〜てはだめ ("must not …") later.

な-adjective/noun, so past = だめでした, negative = だめじゃない.

#### すっかり — `new`

From class 2026-07-18 — sensei used すっかり忘れました. Adverb: *completely /
thoroughly*, with a nuance of a **full change into a new state** (not just
"very"). Pairs with verbs of becoming/finishing/forgetting: すっかり忘れました,
すっかり元気になりました, すっかり春ですね.

Neighbours:
- **すっかり** — completely, with transformation into a new state.
- **完全に (かんぜんに)** — completely, neutral/clinical.
- **全然 (ぜんぜん)** — "(not) at all," usually needs a negative.

### Stretch (N4)

#### Interjections: へえ / え〜 / えっ / ええ — `shaky`

From class 2026-07-18. Similar sounds, different jobs.

- **へえ（〜）** — *impressed*, "didn't know that, interesting!" Reaction to a fun
  fact. NOT English "hey" (false friend — "hey" gets attention; へえ is a
  *response*). Closest: "ohh / huh / TIL." Stretched へえ〜 = more impressed.
- **えっ！** (short, sharp) — *"Huh?! What?!"* startled. えっ！本当ですか？
- **え〜** (stretched, whiny) — *surprise + doubt / reluctance / dismay*:
  え〜、うそでしょ！ / え〜、行きたくない〜 / え〜、もう終わり？
- **ええ** (two-mora, flat — NOT drawn out) — soft polite **"yes"**, warmer than
  うん. ええ、そうです。

Key contrast: **へえ** = "oh cool, TIL" (positive); **え〜** = "ehh, really…?"
(doubt). And watch ええ ("yes") vs え〜 ("ehh") — same kana neighbours, opposite
meanings, told apart by length and pitch.
